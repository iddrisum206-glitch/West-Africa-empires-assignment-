1. Introduction

The Ghana, Mali and Songhai empires were major West African states whose power was closely connected to trade across the Sahara. Gold was one of the most valuable commodities in these networks. Today, parts of the same broad region contain important mineral belts and sedimentary basins that are relevant to mineral and petroleum exploration.

Important distinction: Ancient gold trading does not prove that crude oil exists in the same place. Gold and petroleum form through different geological processes. Historical geography helps identify areas to investigate, but modern geological surveys and exploration data are needed to establish resource potential.

2. The Three West African Empires

c. 6th–13th centuries

Ghana Empire

Its core was in the western Sahel, mainly in areas of present-day southeastern Mauritania and western Mali. Koumbi Saleh is commonly identified with its major royal and commercial centre.

The empire gained wealth by taxing and controlling trans-Saharan trade, including gold and salt. Much of the gold traded north came from gold-producing regions farther south.

c. 13th–16th centuries

Mali Empire

The Mali Empire expanded across a large part of the western Sahel and savanna, including areas of present-day Mali, Guinea and Senegal. Important centres included Niani, Timbuktu and Djenné.

Its prosperity was linked to goldfields such as Bambuk and Bure, as well as trade routes connecting West Africa with North Africa.

c. 15th–16th centuries

Songhai Empire

Songhai grew around the middle Niger River, with Gao as a major political centre. At its height it controlled important parts of present-day Mali and neighbouring areas.

It expanded commercial networks that moved gold, salt and other goods through cities such as Gao and Timbuktu.

Dates and territorial descriptions are approximate. Historical boundaries changed over time and should not be treated as modern national borders.

4. Gold Deposits in Present-Day West Africa

West Africa contains extensive gold mineralisation associated with ancient rocks of the West African Craton. Many important deposits occur in Paleoproterozoic Birimian volcanic and sedimentary rocks. Geological structures, including faults and shear zones, can provide pathways and sites for gold-bearing fluids.

It is the underlying geology—not the existence of an empire—that explains where gold deposits occur.

5. Crude Oil and Petroleum Exploration Potential

Petroleum exploration focuses on sedimentary basins where organic-rich source rocks may have generated hydrocarbons. A working petroleum system also needs suitable reservoir rocks, seals and traps, together with the right burial and temperature history.

The Taoudeni Basin: Mali and Mauritania

The Taoudeni Basin is a very large sedimentary basin extending across much of Mauritania and Mali. It lies within a region that overlaps the broad historical geography of the Ghana and Mali empires and areas later influenced by Songhai. The U.S. Geological Survey assessed the basin's undiscovered oil and gas potential.

160 million barrelsUSGS mean estimate of undiscovered, technically recoverable conventional oil
1,880 billion cubic feetUSGS mean estimate of undiscovered, technically recoverable conventional natural gas

These figures are estimates from the USGS assessment published in 2016, based on 2015 assessment work. They are not confirmed reserves or proof of commercial discoveries.

Other petroleum example: Niger Delta

The Niger Delta, mainly in Nigeria and extending into Cameroon, is a well-known petroleum province. Its petroleum system developed in a thick sedimentary sequence containing source rocks, reservoirs and seals. It is geographically outside the core territories of the three empires, but provides a useful West African comparison showing how sedimentary geology can create major oil and gas provinces.

What petroleum engineers investigate

  • Source rock: organic-rich rock capable of generating oil or gas.
  • Reservoir rock: porous and permeable rock that can store and transmit hydrocarbons.
  • Seal rock: low-permeability rock that prevents hydrocarbons from escaping.
  • Trap: a geological arrangement that allows hydrocarbons to accumulate.
  • Exploration: geological studies, geophysical surveys and drilling to test whether a commercial accumulation exists.

6. Conclusion

The Ghana, Mali and Songhai empires demonstrate the historical importance of West Africa's gold trade. Their former geographical sphere includes areas associated with important modern gold provinces and the Taoudeni sedimentary basin, which has been assessed for undiscovered petroleum resources. However, historical trade routes cannot by themselves predict oil or gold deposits. The presence of these resources is explained by geological conditions and must be investigated using modern geological mapping, geophysical methods, sampling and exploratory drilling.

For petroleum engineering, the key lesson is that understanding the geological history of a region is essential when evaluating its potential energy resources.
